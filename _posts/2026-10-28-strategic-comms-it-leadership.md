In 2024, I was a communications and change management consultant at [Cadmus](https://cadmusgroup.com/), supporting the Applications Development division of a federal agency’s IT organization. The agency was moving to the cloud, and leadership wanted employees to grow their technical skills to get there. But employees weren’t participating in workforce development initiatives, and leadership didn’t have time to find out what employees needed and found useful.

So I started with a communications strategy, then built a small community to bring it to life: the Workforce Development Employee Advisory Group.

## Secure Executive Sponsors

Before recruiting members, I lined up executive sponsors and built a plan. I also invited a lead from the division’s workforce development team to join the group, so we were partnering with the right people from the start and no one was working against each other. Then I wrote the call for volunteers and had the sponsors send it themselves.

The order matters, because without executive sponsorship, a call for volunteers is easy to ignore. The messenger matters as much as the message, and an invitation from leadership tells employees this effort is real. About seven employees raised their hands.

## Ask Questions People Can Answer

I designed and facilitated every session around plain questions, like:

- “Where do you go to find the training you need?”
- “What is holding you back from skill building?”
- “How easy is it to find a mentor here?”

They didn’t hold back:

- They didn’t know where to get the training they needed.
- Java developers had no sandbox to practice in.
- The mentorship program was hard to navigate and didn’t always match people with the right mentor.

## Show Progress Every Time

Volunteers have day jobs, so a community only keeps showing up if every session moves their ideas forward. Over 6+ sessions, I reflected back what we heard (“Here’s what we heard. Is anything missing?”), the group picked the problems that mattered most, and we began solutioning recommendations immediately.

## Empower Employees to Own the Pitch

The group presented their recommendations to the executive sponsors themselves. Employees are invested when there’s structure, guidance, and most importantly, a level of accountability. These were their ideas, so they had to stand behind them.

Most of them weren’t comfortable presenting to executives, so I built the presentation from their own words and ran walkthroughs with them until they felt ready. Leaders heard the problems straight from the people living them, and that carried more weight than any consultant’s slide.

## Pitch It Like a Product Launch

We didn’t just pitch ideas, we pitched plans. Our Teams channel proposal had clear goals, success metrics (monthly active users, engagement, quarterly pulse surveys), a five-week rollout and a promotion plan.

In under three months, leadership approved three initiatives:

- A Microsoft Teams channel to connect employees around workforce development
- A redesigned, rebranded SharePoint site so training was easy to find
- An employee success story series

We knew the Teams channel could work, because our team had already built a [virtual watercooler](/virtual-watercooler.html) in Teams for the agency’s IT organization.

## Follow Through Where People Can See It

Once leadership said yes, I ran an intranet audit, restructured the SharePoint site’s content, and wrote articles. Visits to the SharePoint site went up, and employees started posting in the new Teams channel every week. I also partnered with the division’s workforce development leads to tackle the bigger issues employees raised, like Java sandboxes and mentor matching.

The director also shared our progress at his monthly Ask Me Anything sessions, so the whole organization could see that speaking up led somewhere. Employees finally had a direct line to the people who could change things. Leadership also had a clearer path to getting their teams ready for the cloud.

## What I Learned

Strategic communications isn’t just what you say, it’s who says it, when you say it, and what happens after.

Creating a group to fix organizational problems doesn’t work on its own, but designing an environment where people feel empowered does. I learned that [leading the Clemson Young Alumni of Charleston](/young-alumni-community-building.html), and it held true here. Community building and strategic communications work best together, and when they do, great leaders listen.
